
# Female Exercises — Physiological Measurements

## Purpose

This reference documents the scientific evidence relevant to the four female exercises discussed in Measurable Science: breast massage, nipple stimulation, vaginal resistance training, and identification of an individually sensitive vaginal area.

The studies investigate separate physiological effects. None validates the four exercises as a complete system or demonstrates their proposed spiritual outcome.

## 1. Breast massage

Kang, K. A., Lee, J. A., and Hur, M. H. (2022).
Effects of periodic breast massage on the cyclical mastalgia of fertile women before their menstrual period.

https://doi.org/10.15207/JKCS.2022.13.03.353

This randomized controlled study enrolled 54 women with cyclical breast pain. The intervention involved 30 minutes of massage, divided equally between the breasts.

Researchers measured pain, breast hardness, nipple extensibility, breast size, and blood circulation.

Significant differences were reported for several outcomes. The between-group blood circulation result was statistically significant for the left breast, but not the right.

The study does not establish that 60 circular movements three times daily increase estrogen, permanently lift the breasts, or produce the same effects in healthy women without cyclical breast pain.

## 2. Nipple stimulation, oxytocin, and uterine contractions

Christensson et al. (1989).
Effect of nipple stimulation on uterine activity and on plasma levels of oxytocin in full term, healthy, pregnant women.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2618602/

Ten healthy women at 38–39 weeks of pregnancy stimulated their nipples for 30 minutes.

Nine experienced uterine contractions. Plasma oxytocin increased significantly, and brief oxytocin pulses were recorded in association with contractions.

One participant experienced uterine hyperactivity and fetal heart-rate decelerations.

Mashini et al. (1987).
Comparison of uterine activity induced by nipple stimulation and oxytocin.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3796923/

This study involved 45 pregnant women at term: 25 received intermittent nipple stimulation and 20 received oxytocin infusion.

Uterine activity was measured using internal tocodynamometry. Both groups showed increased activity.

Three women in the nipple-stimulation group experienced uterine hyperstimulation.

These studies support a physiological relationship between nipple stimulation, oxytocin release, and uterine activity during late pregnancy.

They do not establish that a nonpregnant woman can learn to reproduce uterine contractions voluntarily, or that painful nipple traction is necessary.

The reported adverse events also mean that intensive nipple stimulation during pregnancy cannot be assumed safe.

## 3. Vaginal cones and pelvic-floor training

Herbison and Dean (2013).
Weighted vaginal cones for urinary incontinence.

Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews.

https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD002114_vaginal-weights-training-pelvic-floor-muscles-treat-urinary-incontinence-women

The review included 23 trials involving 1,806 women, of whom 717 received vaginal-cone treatment.

Weighted vaginal cones performed better than no active treatment for stress urinary incontinence. No clear advantage over conventional pelvic-floor muscle training was established.

The studies support the use of vaginal resistance devices for pelvic-floor training.

They do not demonstrate any special property of stone Yoni eggs or validate their specific material, shape, hygiene, or safety.

## 4. The G-spot and individual vaginal sensitivity

Vieira-Baptista et al. (2021).
G-spot: Fact or Fiction? A Systematic Review.

https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esxm.2021.100435

The review identified 31 eligible studies investigating the existence, location, and anatomical nature of the proposed G-spot.

Across the surveyed populations, 62.9% of women reported having a G-spot. Clinical studies identified a proposed G-spot in approximately 55.4% of women.

Anatomical and imaging findings were inconsistent. A single, universally identifiable G-spot structure remains unproven.

This evidence is compatible with investigating individual differences in vaginal sensitivity.

It does not establish that sustained attention to a personally sensitive area produces the transformation proposed in Measurable Science.

## Conclusion

The available evidence documents physiological effects associated with breast massage, nipple stimulation, and vaginal resistance training, alongside substantial variation and uncertainty concerning vaginal sensitivity.

My proposed sequence and its final objective remain separate hypotheses. The studies should not be presented as experiments on the complete method.
