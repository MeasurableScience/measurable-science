# Reference 044a — Hydrothermal water-rock reactions and fluid salinity

## Purpose

This reference examines a simple physical question relevant to the model
developed in the book:

# What happens chemically when water is heated and circulated through hot
# volcanic rock?

Unlike hypotheses concerning water hundreds of kilometres below the Earth's
surface, hydrothermal fluids at the seafloor can be directly sampled.

The measurements presented here therefore come from actual recovered fluids.

This reference does NOT establish that hydrothermal circulation is the
origin of ocean salinity.

It establishes the narrower experimentally observed fact that:

# water circulating through hot volcanic crust can undergo major chemical
# changes and emerge carrying dissolved material derived from water-rock
# interaction.

---

# 1. Seawater becomes hydrothermal fluid

NOAA researchers studying submarine hydrothermal systems describe the basic
process directly:

seawater penetrates cracks in the oceanic crust, is heated below the
seafloor, reacts with the surrounding rock and subsequently returns toward
the seafloor as hydrothermal fluid.

Butterfield et al. summarize the process:

"Seawater evolves into hydrothermal fluid through a series of reactions as
it is heated below the seafloor."

Therefore the water entering such a system does not necessarily return with
the same chemical composition.

---

# 2. The fluids have been directly sampled

This is important for the methodology used in the book.

These conclusions are not based only on seismic interpretation.

Hydrothermal fluids have been physically collected from active vents using
deep-sea submersibles and specialized sampling equipment.

At the southern Juan de Fuca Ridge, fluids from multiple hydrothermal vents
were sampled and their dissolved elements measured.

Measured elements included:

- lithium — Li
- sodium — Na
- potassium — K
- rubidium — Rb
- calcium — Ca
- strontium — Sr
- barium — Ba
- manganese — Mn
- iron — Fe
- zinc — Zn
- silicon — Si

Additional measurements in hydrothermal studies include:

- chloride — Cl
- magnesium — Mg
- sulfate
- sulfide
- pH
- temperature

The chemical composition of the recovered fluids differed substantially
from ordinary seawater.

---

# 3. Direct evidence of water-rock interaction

Measurements from submarine hydrothermal systems show that heated water
reacting with volcanic rock can lose some dissolved components and acquire
others.

NOAA measurements from the CoAxial hydrothermal system found fluids with:

- depleted Mg;
- elevated Li;
- elevated Ca;
- elevated Fe;
- elevated Mn;
- elevated Si.

The researchers interpret these chemical changes as evidence of
high-temperature water-rock reactions.

At some sites the fluids had experienced extensive water-rock interaction
before returning through fractures to the seafloor.

Therefore:

# hot water moving through volcanic rock is chemically transformed by that
# passage.

---

# 4. Chloride and salinity can change dramatically

One particularly relevant observation concerns chloride.

Chloride is the dominant anion in seawater and is therefore closely related
to seawater salinity.

Hydrothermal fluids sampled from the southern Juan de Fuca Ridge were found
to contain unusually high chloride concentrations.

Von Damm and Bischoff reported fluids with chloride concentrations reaching:

# approximately twice the seawater value.

The authors considered several possible mechanisms, including formation and
mixing of subsurface brines and dissolution of chloride-rich phases.

The precise mechanism was not uniquely determined.

The measurement itself, however, is direct:

# hydrothermal circulation can produce fluids whose chloride concentration
# differs greatly from the seawater that entered the system.

---

# 5. Hydrothermal brines

NOAA measurements from the CoAxial segment provide another example.

At the Source site, fluids venting at approximately:

223–294 °C

were identified as brines with approximately:

695 mmol/kg chloride.

These fluids also contained:

- high Ca;
- high Li;
- Fe;
- Zn;
- dissolved silica;

and showed chemical signatures interpreted as extensive water-rock
interaction.

The researchers concluded that the brine had formed at temperatures above
approximately 400 °C before cooling or mixing and eventually venting at the
seafloor.

Thus a measured natural sequence exists:

seawater
↓
penetration into fractured volcanic crust
↓
heating
↓
water-rock reaction
↓
chemical transformation
↓
formation of hydrothermal fluid / brine
↓
return toward the surface.

---

# 6. A modern measured example

The Jøtul hydrothermal field provides a recent directly sampled example.

Measured black-smoker fluids had temperatures around:

316 °C

with approximately:

Cl = 530 mM
Na = 399 mM
Ca = 37.3 mM

and strongly modified concentrations of several additional dissolved
components.

The hydrothermal endmember was essentially depleted in magnesium compared
with normal seawater.

The measurements demonstrate again that circulation through a hot geological
system can strongly reorganize the chemical composition of the entering
water.

---

# 7. What does this establish?

The observations establish that:

1. water can penetrate fractured volcanic crust;

2. it can circulate through hot rock;

3. it can reach temperatures of hundreds of degrees Celsius;

4. it reacts chemically with the surrounding rock;

5. dissolved elemental concentrations change during this process;

6. the resulting fluid can become enriched in some elements and depleted in
   others;

7. chloride concentration can change substantially;

8. natural hydrothermal brines can form;

9. the chemically altered fluid can subsequently return to the ocean.

These processes have been observed through direct sampling of natural
hydrothermal systems.

---

# 8. What does this NOT establish?

These measurements do NOT establish that:

- all ocean salt originated in a deep hydrothermal system;
- the proposed geological reactor beneath the Black Rock existed;
- the proposed 9 Sv water flow passes through such a system;
- hydrothermal fluids alone produced the modern chemical composition of the
  oceans;
- the deep circulation proposed in the book follows the same geometry as
  modern seafloor hydrothermal systems.

Those remain separate questions.

The experimentally supported statement required here is narrower:

# water passing through hot fractured volcanic rock can return as a
# chemically altered, mineral-bearing and in some cases highly saline fluid.

---

# Relevance to the model

The previous reference established that ionizing radiation can alter water
through radiolysis.

This reference establishes a second independent process.

Water passing through the proposed system would not interact only with
radiation.

It would also interact with:

- hot volcanic rock;
- minerals;
- fractures;
- high temperatures;
- pressure;
- and potentially magmatic material.

Natural hydrothermal systems demonstrate that such interaction can strongly
modify water chemistry.

Therefore the question raised in the book is physically testable:

# Could prolonged passage through the proposed deep geological system
# contribute to the salinity and chemical composition of the water that
# eventually returns to the ocean?

This reference does not assume the answer.

It establishes that the required first step —

# chemical transformation of water through hot rock —

occurs in directly sampled natural systems.

# Conclusion

Direct sampling of submarine hydrothermal systems demonstrates the physical
sequence:

water
→ hot fractured rock
→ heating
→ water-rock reaction
→ chemical transformation
→ mineral-bearing hydrothermal fluid / brine
→ return to the ocean.

The measurements therefore provide an experimental basis for investigating
the chemical fate of water passing through the geological system proposed
in the book.
