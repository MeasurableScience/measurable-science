# Reference 027 — Mathematical model of the first breakthrough

## Purpose

This file contains the simplified mathematics used in the book to test
the proposed first high-pressure magmatic breakthrough.

It is not presented as a reconstruction of a known volcanic eruption.

The calculation asks:

1. What pressure head is available from a magma source at 250–300 km depth?
2. What ideal exit velocity follows from that pressure difference?
3. What vertical and horizontal ballistic range follows in the absence
   of an atmosphere?
4. What volume of magma could theoretically pass through a 47 km diameter
   opening per second?
5. How much ice could the thermal energy of that magma theoretically melt?
6. If the erupted material were distributed over its ideal ballistic
   footprint, what average thickness would one second of eruption produce?

---

# 1. Assumptions

Depth of source:

h = 250–300 km

Mean density of surrounding deep rock:

rho_r = 3300 kg/m^3

Density of magma:

rho_m = 2700 kg/m^3

Acceleration due to gravity:

g = 9.81 m/s^2

Working diameter of central opening derived from the Mercator description:

D = 47 km

Radius:

r = 23.5 km

Atmospheric drag:

none

Friction, conduit losses, fragmentation, cooling during ascent and
three-dimensional flow losses:

ignored

The results therefore represent idealised upper limits rather than
predictions of an actual eruption.

---

# 2. Pressure difference driving the magma

The surrounding material and the magma column do not have the same density.

The ideal pressure difference is approximated by:

Delta P = (rho_r - rho_m) g h

For h = 250 km:

Delta P
= (3300 - 2700)(9.81)(250000)

Delta P = 1.4715 x 10^9 Pa

or:

~1.47 GPa

For h = 300 km:

Delta P
= (3300 - 2700)(9.81)(300000)

Delta P = 1.7658 x 10^9 Pa

or:

~1.77 GPa

Thus the simplified buoyancy / pressure-head model gives:

~1.5–1.8 GPa

of effective pressure difference.

---

# 3. Ideal exit velocity

If the available pressure difference is converted into kinetic energy:

v = sqrt(2 Delta P / rho_m)

For 250 km depth:

v ≈ 1044 m/s

or:

~1.04 km/s

For 300 km depth:

v ≈ 1144 m/s

or:

~1.14 km/s

Therefore the ideal exit velocity is of order:

~1.0–1.15 km/s

before adding any additional contribution from gas expansion.

---

# 4. Maximum vertical rise

For ballistic motion in vacuum:

H = v^2 / (2g)

For the 250 km source:

H ≈ 55.6 km

For the 300 km source:

H ≈ 66.7 km

Therefore the simplified model predicts a maximum vertical ballistic
height of approximately:

~56–67 km

above the original surface.

This value follows from the pressure-head calculation itself and is not
an assumed 80 km plume height.

---

# 5. Maximum horizontal ballistic range

For a projectile launched at the ideal 45 degree angle in vacuum:

R = v^2 / g

This is twice the corresponding maximum vertical height.

For the 250 km source:

R ≈ 111 km

For the 300 km source:

R ≈ 133 km

Therefore material launched ballistically at the optimum angle could
reach approximately:

~110–135 km from the central source

in this simplified atmosphere-free model.

This does not include later surface flow, underground magma transport,
water transport or subsequent eruptions.

---

# 6. Area of the 47 km opening

A = pi r^2

A = pi (23.5 km)^2

A ≈ 1735 km^2

or:

1.735 x 10^9 m^2

---

# 7. Ideal volumetric eruption rate

Using:

Q = A v

For the 250 km source:

Q ≈ 1.811 x 10^12 m^3/s

or:

~1810 km^3/s

For the 300 km source:

Q ≈ 1.984 x 10^12 m^3/s

or:

~1980 km^3/s

Therefore, if the entire 47 km diameter cross-section were simultaneously
open and filled with magma moving at the ideal calculated velocity, the
upper-limit flow rate would be of order:

~1800–2000 km^3 of magma per second.

This is an extreme mathematical upper bound.

It does not imply that a real 47 km structure would behave as a fully open
cylindrical conduit.

---

# 8. Mass flow

Using:

mass flow = rho_m Q

For 250 km depth:

~4.9 x 10^15 kg/s

For 300 km depth:

~5.4 x 10^15 kg/s

Thus the idealised mass flow is of order:

~5 x 10^15 kg/s.

---

# 9. Thermal energy available from cooling magma

For a first-order basaltic estimate, use:

specific heat capacity:

c_p ≈ 1046 J/kg/K

initial lava temperature:

T ≈ 1150 C

latent heat of crystallisation:

L ≈ 418,000 J/kg

Published thermal models of basaltic magma use values of this order.

Approximate thermal energy released while cooling and crystallising:

E_m = c_p Delta T + L

E_m
≈ (1046)(1150) + 418000

E_m ≈ 1.62 x 10^6 J/kg

or:

~1.62 MJ/kg of magma.

References for representative magma thermal properties include:

- specific heat capacity of magma around 1200 J/kg/K and latent heat of
  crystallisation around 350 kJ/kg in modern dike-propagation models;
- basalt lava values near 1046 J/kg/K and 418 kJ/kg for crystallisation.

---

# 10. Maximum amount of ice melted

For an upper-bound calculation, first assume that the ice is already at
its melting point.

Latent heat of fusion of ice:

L_ice ≈ 333.5 kJ/kg

Ice density:

rho_ice ≈ 917 kg/m^3

If all thermal energy from the erupted magma were transferred into melting
ice, then:

For the 250 km case:

thermal power ≈ 7.93 x 10^21 J/s

which could melt approximately:

2.38 x 10^16 kg of ice per second

or approximately:

~25,900 km^3 of ice per second.

For the 300 km case:

thermal power ≈ 8.68 x 10^21 J/s

which could melt approximately:

2.60 x 10^16 kg of ice per second

or approximately:

~28,400 km^3 of ice per second.

Therefore the theoretical thermal upper limit is approximately:

~26,000–28,000 km^3 of ice melted per second.

IMPORTANT:

This assumes ice already at 0 C and 100 percent heat transfer from magma
to ice.

Colder ice, steam production, heating of water and surrounding rock,
radiative losses and incomplete contact would substantially reduce the
actual melted volume.

This result is therefore an absolute upper-bound energy calculation.

---

# 11. Including impact / kinetic energy as a limiting upper bound

The calculated kinetic energy per kilogram of magma is approximately:

250 km source:

~0.545 MJ/kg

300 km source:

~0.654 MJ/kg

If, unrealistically, all of this kinetic energy were also converted into
heat in the ice in addition to the magma's thermal energy, the absolute
energy ceiling would correspond to roughly:

~34,600 km^3/s of ice

for the 250 km case,

and:

~39,900 km^3/s

for the 300 km case.

These values are not expected physical melt rates.

They merely establish an extreme conservation-of-energy ceiling.

---

# 12. Area reached by the first ballistic ejecta

Using the maximum ideal ballistic radius:

250 km source:

R ≈ 111 km

Surface area inside that radius:

A_250 = pi R^2

≈ 38,800 km^2

300 km source:

R ≈ 133 km

Surface area:

A_300 ≈ 55,900 km^2

Thus the first purely ballistic deposition field in this simplified model
has a diameter of approximately:

~220–270 km.

This is separate from the much larger approximately 880 km polar
depression described in the Mercator material.

---

# 13. Maximum average relief added per second

This calculation asks only:

If one second of the ideal magma discharge were spread uniformly over the
entire corresponding ballistic footprint, what average thickness would it
produce?

For the 250 km case:

1811 km^3 / 38,785 km^2

≈ 0.0467 km

or:

~47 metres per second.

For the 300 km case:

1984 km^3 / 55,851 km^2

≈ 0.0355 km

or:

~36 metres per second.

Therefore one second of the idealised maximum eruption, if deposited
uniformly over the complete ballistic footprint, corresponds to an average
layer of approximately:

~35–50 metres.

One minute at the same theoretical rate would correspond to approximately:

~2.1–2.8 km average thickness.

This is not a prediction of real topography.

Real ejecta would be highly nonuniform, most material would not be launched
at the optimum ballistic angle, large quantities could fall back into the
central depression, and molten material could continue to flow after
landing.

The calculation is included only to establish the scale implied by the
assumed opening and ideal velocity.

# 15. Interpretation limits

The calculation does not prove that such an eruption occurred.

It tests the mathematical consequences of combining:

- a 250–300 km deep source,
- the assumed density contrast,
- a 47 km diameter central structure,
- and an atmosphere-free surface.

The calculated discharge is an extreme upper limit because the model
assumes the entire cross-section is simultaneously active and ignores
friction, cooling during ascent, conduit geometry, fragmentation and
energy losses.

The ice-melting calculation is likewise an upper energy bound.

The values are intended to allow the hypothesis to make quantitative
predictions that can later be compared with the physical consequences
required elsewhere in the model.
