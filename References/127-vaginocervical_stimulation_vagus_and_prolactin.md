
# Vaginocervical Stimulation, Vagus Nerve, and Prolactin

## Purpose

This reference documents three experimental findings relevant to the vaginal-orgasm chapter of Measurable Science:

1. Distinguishable sensory cortical responses to stimulation of the clitoris, vagina, and cervix.
2. Evidence of a vagus-mediated sensory pathway in women with spinal cord injuries.
3. An approximately fivefold greater post-orgasmic prolactin increase following penile-vaginal intercourse than following masturbation.

The central research question is how genital sensory signals reach the brain and how neural, hormonal, and bodily responses are organized during orgasm.

The relationship between two partners is a separate hypothesis that requires simultaneous measurements of both participants.

---

## 1. Genital sensory representations

Komisaruk, B. R., Wise, N., Frangos, E., Liu, W.-C., Allen, K., & Brody, S. (2011).

Women's clitoris, vagina, and cervix mapped on the sensory cortex: fMRI evidence.

The Journal of Sexual Medicine, 8(10), 2822–2830.

DOI:
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1743-6109.2011.02388.x

Full text:
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3186818/

PMID: 21797981

### Experimental method

Eleven healthy women underwent functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) during separate self-stimulation of the clitoris, vagina, cervix, and nipple.

Thumb and great-toe stimulation provided additional reference conditions.

### Measurements

Clitoral, vaginal, and cervical stimulation activated distinguishable but closely situated regions of the medial sensory cortex.

Nipple stimulation activated both the thoracic sensory representation and the genital sensory cortex.

### Relevance

The findings demonstrate that stimulation of the vagina and cervix produces identifiable brain responses that can be distinguished from responses to clitoral stimulation.

These are measurements of sensory cortical activity, not a comparison of different orgasm types.

## 2. Vaginocervical stimulation and the vagus nerve

Komisaruk, B. R., Whipple, B., Crawford, A., Liu, W.-C., Kalnin, A., & Mosier, K. (2004).

Brain activation during vaginocervical self-stimulation and orgasm in women with complete spinal cord injury: fMRI evidence of mediation by the vagus nerves.

Brain Research, 1024(1–2), 77–88.

DOI:
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brainres.2004.07.029

PubMed:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15451368/

### Research question

Can sensory information from the vagina and cervix reach the brain through the vagus nerves, bypassing the spinal cord?

### Participants

Five women with spinal cord injuries at or above T10 participated.

Four had complete spinal cord injuries according to ASIA criteria.

One had a significant but incomplete injury.

### Experimental method

Researchers used fMRI to measure brain activity during vaginocervical self-stimulation.

They examined the nucleus tractus solitarii (NTS), a brainstem region receiving sensory projections from the vagus nerves.

Additional sensory and motor procedures helped identify the relevant brainstem structures.

### Measurements

All five women showed activation in the inferior NTS region during vaginocervical stimulation.

All five also demonstrated stimulation-associated analgesia measured at the fingers.

Three women experienced orgasm.

Orgasm-associated activation included the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus, medial amygdala, anterior cingulate cortex, frontal and parietal cortices, insula, and cerebellum.

### Interpretation

The findings support a vagus-mediated pathway for vaginal and cervical sensory information that bypasses the spinal cord.

The experiment is particularly important because genital sensation and orgasm occurred despite major interruption of the conventional spinal pathways.

It provides evidence for an alternative sensory route but does not directly record electrical impulses traveling through the vagus nerve.

Nor does it establish that the entire orgasmic process is transmitted through this nerve alone.

### Relevance to Measurable Science

The vagus nerve is a specific physiological pathway that can be investigated when studying the relationship between genital stimulation and brain activity.

The experiment provides a measurable neural component of orgasm-related physiology, distinct from subsequent muscular, cardiovascular, and hormonal responses.

## 3. Post-orgasmic prolactin: intercourse versus masturbation

Brody, S., & Krüger, T. H. C. (2006).

The post-orgasmic prolactin increase following intercourse is greater than following masturbation and suggests greater satiety.

Biological Psychology, 71(3), 312–315.

DOI:
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsycho.2005.06.008

PubMed:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16095799/

### Research question

Does the post-orgasmic prolactin response differ between masturbation and penile-vaginal intercourse?

### Experimental basis

The authors compared data from three laboratory studies involving men and women who experienced orgasm during masturbation or penile-vaginal intercourse.

Prolactin changes were adjusted for changes observed during nonsexual control conditions.

### Measurements

The magnitude of the post-orgasmic prolactin increase following intercourse was approximately 400% greater than following masturbation.

This corresponds to an approximately fivefold difference in the measured increase.

It does not mean that total prolactin concentration was five times higher.

The difference was reported for both sexes.

### Relevance to partnered sexual activity

The experiment demonstrates that the hormonal response following orgasm can differ substantially between partnered intercourse and masturbation.

This finding is relevant to the book's emphasis on relationships and interactions between two people.

However, the experiment did not independently isolate the effects of partner presence, emotional closeness, oxytocin, penetration, or differences in stimulation.

It therefore does not identify which factor produced the observed prolactin difference.

## 4. Relationship hypothesis

The preceding chapters describe separate experimental observations:

- Inter-brain coordination during romantic kissing.
- Increased oxytocin during mechanical breast stimulation.
- Distinguishable genital sensory pathways.
- Vagus-associated brainstem activation during vaginocervical stimulation.
- A substantially greater prolactin increase after intercourse than after masturbation.

Together, these findings motivate an investigation of sexual interaction as a process involving two people rather than an isolated response of one body.

The proposed relationship between these findings is a hypothesis, not a result established by any single experiment.

A direct investigation would require simultaneous recordings from both partners, synchronized across the successive stages of sexual interaction.

## Evidence classification

### MEASUREMENT

- fMRI activation of distinguishable genital sensory cortical regions.
- Inferior NTS activation during vaginocervical stimulation in five women with spinal cord injuries.
- Orgasm in three participants in the spinal cord injury experiment.
- An approximately fivefold greater post-orgasmic prolactin increase following intercourse than following masturbation.

### MATHEMATICAL ANALYSIS

- Statistical analysis of fMRI activation patterns.
- Comparison of brain activity across stimulation conditions.
- Comparison of prolactin changes after adjustment for nonsexual control conditions.

### HYPOTHESIS

Sexual interaction between two partners may produce coordinated neural and hormonal responses that cannot be fully characterized by measuring either participant in isolation.

The vagus nerve may contribute to the neural processes involved in vaginocervical sensation and orgasm.

The observed prolactin difference motivates further investigation of partnered interaction but does not establish oxytocin or emotional bonding as its cause.

## Conclusion

The cited experiments establish distinguishable genital sensory representations, support a vagus-mediated pathway for vaginocervical sensation, and demonstrate a substantial difference in post-orgasmic prolactin responses between intercourse and masturbation.

They provide independent physiological measurements relevant to the relationship-centered hypothesis developed in Measurable Science.
