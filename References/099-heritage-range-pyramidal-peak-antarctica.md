## 3. Pyramidal morphology

The mountain is notable for its sharply defined pyramidal appearance.

Exposed rock faces surrounded by ice produce several triangular
surfaces converging toward the summit.

Pyramidal mountain forms are not geologically unique.

Similar forms can be produced when erosion acts on several sides of a
mountain peak.

Geologists cited in published discussion of this Antarctic formation
interpret it as a natural mountain shaped by erosion rather than as an
artificial pyramid.

Mauri Pelto, professor of environmental science at Nichols College,
identified freeze-thaw erosion as one mechanism capable of enlarging
fractures and progressively shaping exposed rock.

Eric Rignot, professor of Earth system science at the University of
California, Irvine, likewise described the formation as a mountain
whose shape resembles a pyramid.

## 4. Geographic context

The Heritage Range forms part of the Ellsworth Mountains of West
Antarctica.

The Ellsworth Mountains were first sighted by Lincoln Ellsworth during
his trans-Antarctic flight on 23 November 1935.

The Heritage Range was subsequently explored, photographed and mapped
during later Antarctic expeditions and U.S. geological programs.

The region contains numerous exposed peaks and nunataks surrounded by
glacial ice.

A nunatak is an exposed rocky summit or ridge projecting above an ice
sheet or glacier.

The pyramidal formation discussed here is one such exposed mountain
structure.
