# Electromagnetic Fields in Living Organisms and Geological Structures: Measurements and the EM Envelope Hypothesis

## Purpose and terminology

This reference documents experimentally measured electromagnetic phenomena associated with biological organisms and geological structures. In the *Measurable Science* model, the term **EM envelope** (*EM opna*) is proposed for the electromagnetic organization associated with an organized physical form. The term is conceptually related to what is sometimes called an **aura**, but it is **not** used here as an experimentally established, sharply bounded field or as evidence of a spiritual aura.

The central distinction is between **measured physical fields** and the **additional hypothesis** that their spatial and temporal organization could help identify and track a particular form as it changes.

## 1. Human heart: magnetic fields outside the body

David Cohen investigated magnetic fields produced by the electrical activity of the human heart and measured signals outside the torso in a shielded environment (1967). Cohen, Edelsack and Zimmerman subsequently recorded magnetocardiograms using a superconducting point-contact magnetometer (1970). These experiments establish that organized physiological electrical activity can generate externally measurable magnetic fields.

They do not establish a stable, unique electromagnetic boundary around the entire person or show that such a field is a persistent identifier.

## 2. Human brain: measurable magnetic activity

Cohen (1968) reported weak alternating magnetic fields outside the scalp associated with the brain's alpha rhythm. In 1972 he demonstrated direct recording of the brain's magnetic activity with a superconducting magnetometer in a shielded room. This work provides experimental support for spatially distributed, time-varying magnetic activity arising from the nervous system.

The measured signal depends on neuronal activity, sensor position, measurement conditions and time. It should not be equated with a permanent electromagnetic portrait of a person.

## 3. Plants: biomagnetic signals from electrical excitation

Fabricant and colleagues (2021) measured biomagnetic signals associated with electrically induced action potentials in the Venus flytrap (*Dionaea muscipula*). The experiment used optically pumped atomic magnetometers and controlled stimulation, with electrical recordings accompanying magnetic measurements.

This extends the experimentally demonstrated occurrence of biomagnetic signals beyond humans and other animals. It does not imply that every plant has a measurable whole-organism envelope under arbitrary conditions.

## 4. Magnetotactic bacteria: organized magnetic structures in living cells

Magnetotactic bacteria synthesize magnetosomes containing magnetic mineral crystals, often organized in chains. These structures allow the organisms to respond to geomagnetic fields. Reviews by Uebe and Schüler (2016) and Schüler and colleagues (2026) describe their structure, biosynthesis and function.

These findings show that biological organization can include persistent, physically structured magnetic components. They do not establish a universal mechanism of electromagnetic identity across all life.

## 5. Geological structures: induced and remanent magnetization

Geological materials can carry both induced and remanent magnetization. The U.S. Geological Survey explains that magnetized crustal rocks, particularly those containing magnetic minerals such as magnetite, produce spatial magnetic anomalies that can be mapped by aeromagnetic surveys. Remanent magnetization may preserve aspects of a rock's magnetic history.

Thus geological structures can possess measurable magnetic characteristics related to their mineralogy, geometry and history. Such anomalies are not equivalent to biological electrical activity, nor do they demonstrate a universal, bounded 'aura' around each geological object.

## 6. Interpretation within the EM envelope hypothesis

The cited experiments support the following limited physical observations:

1. Some living systems generate externally measurable magnetic fields through electrical activity.
2. Some living systems contain organized magnetic structures.
3. Some geological structures exhibit measurable induced and remanent magnetization and associated magnetic anomalies.
4. Electromagnetic measurements may vary with internal state, structure, orientation, environment and time.

The proposed **EM envelope** goes beyond these observations. It hypothesizes that the electromagnetic organization of a physical form may contain relational features that remain sufficiently continuous through change to assist in identifying that form.

This is an **untested proposal**, not a conclusion demonstrated by the cited papers. In particular, no cited experiment establishes that every organism or geological structure possesses a unique, stable and independently decodable electromagnetic identity signature.

## 7. What would need to be measured

A direct test of the identity hypothesis would require repeated, spatially resolved electromagnetic measurements of the same object across time, changing states and positions; measurements of other similar objects; environmental and instrumental controls; and a **predefined blind identification procedure**.

A useful result would have to distinguish the same object from others better than appropriate controls while remaining robust to changes in state and environment. Detecting an electromagnetic field alone would not meet this criterion.

## Conclusion

**Electromagnetic phenomena associated with biological activity and geological materials are measurable physical facts.** The stronger claim that each organized form possesses a persistent, individually identifiable **EM envelope** remains a hypothesis requiring direct, controlled tests. The term *aura* in this manuscript is a conceptual analogy, not an experimentally validated synonym for a universal electromagnetic identity field.
