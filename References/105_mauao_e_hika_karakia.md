# Māori Sun Traditions — Mauao and Māui

## Purpose

This reference documents two Māori traditions connected with the Sun:

1. the story of **Mauao**, the nameless mountain hauled toward the Pacific Ocean during the night until the arrival of dawn;
2. the story of **Māui slowing the Sun**, in which Māui and his brothers use ropes made from harakeke to restrain the Sun and change the length of the day.

The reference also documents the traditional karakia **E Hika Tū Ake**, chanted by the patupaiarehe while hauling Mauao toward the ocean.

These traditions are presented here as Māori source material. Their later interpretation within *Measurable Science* is treated separately.

---

## 1. Mauao — Caught by the Dawn

According to the Tauranga Moana tradition, Mauao was originally a nameless mountain living among the hills and forests of Hautere.

The nameless mountain was a **pononga**, a servant or slave, of the great chiefly mountain **Ōtānewainuku**.

Nearby stood the beautiful female mountain **Pūwhenua**.

The nameless mountain fell deeply in love with Pūwhenua, but her heart already belonged to Ōtānewainuku.

Overcome by grief, the nameless mountain decided that he no longer wished to live. He resolved to travel to **Te Moananui-a-Kiwa — the Pacific Ocean — and drown himself**.

He called upon the **patupaiarehe**, supernatural beings associated with the forests and the night, and asked them to help him reach the sea.

The patupaiarehe accepted his request.

They plaited great ropes, fastened them around the mountain and began hauling him through the darkness toward the ocean.

As they pulled the mountain, they chanted a karakia.

---

## E Hika Tū Ake — The Karakia of Mauao

The traditional chant associated with the hauling of Mauao is known as **E Hika Tū Ake**.

The karakia describes movement, the journey toward the sea and the approaching end of the night.

Among its lines are:

> **E hika tū ake**

and:

> **Hiki nuku, hiki rangi**

Published translations render this section as a call to rise, pull and **move heaven and earth**.

As the chant continues, the sea becomes increasingly present through the salt spray and the sound of the waves.

The karakia then introduces an explicit celestial reference:

> **Horahia ō mata ki a Meremere Tūahiahi**

**Meremere Tūahiahi** is identified as **Venus, the Evening Star**.

The following line connects the celestial marker with the path toward the ocean:

> **Hei taki i te ara ki a Tangaroa**

Tangaroa is associated with the sea, and published translations describe the Evening Star as showing or illuminating the way toward the ocean.

The sequence preserved in the chant is therefore:

**night → movement → sea → Meremere Tūahiahi / Venus → Tangaroa / ocean → awakening birds → dawn**

Near the end of the chant, the birds awaken and dawn arrives.

The patupaiarehe, beings of the night, can no longer continue their work and abandon the mountain at the edge of the sea.

The nameless mountain therefore never completes his intended journey into the Pacific.

He remains where the morning light has caught him.

From that event he receives the name:

**Mauao — the mountain caught by the dawn / morning light.**

---

## Waimapu — The Path Left Behind

The tradition also connects the journey of Mauao with the physical landscape of Tauranga Moana.

As the patupaiarehe hauled the mountain from the inland hills toward the sea, his movement carved a path through the land.

This path is associated with the valley and waterway now known as **Waimapu**.

The Tauranga Moana Iwi Management Plan describes the associated waiata / tauparapara as acknowledging the connection between **the mountains, the sea and everything in between**.

The landscape is therefore part of the tradition itself: the journey is not described only through characters and events, but is connected with identifiable places that remain in Tauranga Moana.

---

# 2. Māui Slows the Sun

A second Māori tradition deals directly with the movement of the Sun.

According to the story, in the time of Māui the Sun travelled across the sky too quickly.

The days were so short that people did not have enough daylight to complete their work and carry out the activities necessary for life.

Māui decided that the movement of the Sun had to be changed.

He gathered his brothers and explained his plan.

They prepared strong ropes and nooses made from **harakeke — New Zealand flax**.

Māui and his brothers then travelled eastward toward the place from which the Sun emerged.

They journeyed by night so that the Sun would not see what they were doing.

When they reached the place of sunrise, they constructed hiding places and arranged the ropes in preparation for the Sun's appearance.

They waited for dawn.

When the Sun began to rise, Māui's brothers threw the ropes around it and pulled them tight.

The Sun struggled against the restraints, but the brothers held the ropes while Māui attacked it.

Māui carried a powerful ancestral weapon:

**the jawbone of his grandmother, Murirangawhenua.**

With this inherited jawbone, Māui struck the Sun repeatedly while his brothers continued to restrain it with the harakeke ropes.

Eventually the Sun could no longer continue its former rapid movement.

Its passage across the sky was slowed.

From then on, according to the tradition, the Sun travelled more slowly and the days became long enough for people to work, gather food and live their lives.

---

## The Jawbone of Murirangawhenua

The weapon used by Māui is not an arbitrary object.

In Māori traditions, Māui obtains the jawbone from his grandmother **Murirangawhenua**, and it appears as an ancestral object of power in several of his exploits.

In the story of slowing the Sun, the jawbone is the instrument with which Māui acts upon the restrained Sun.

The source tradition therefore preserves three distinct elements:

**harakeke ropes → restraint**

**ancestral jawbone → action upon the Sun**

**slower solar movement → longer day**

The interpretation of the ancestral jawbone as representing **inherited knowledge from the ancestors** belongs to the model proposed in *Measurable Science* and should not be confused with an explicit statement made by the traditional sources.

---

## A Shared Motif: Ropes, Dawn and the Sun

The two traditions are separate stories, but several elements appear in both.

In the Mauao tradition:

- powerful ropes are made;
- a large object is moved during the night;
- the journey is oriented through celestial and geographical markers;
- the operation ends with the arrival of dawn.

In the Māui tradition:

- strong ropes are made from harakeke;
- the operation is prepared during the night;
- the participants wait at the place of sunrise;
- the ropes restrain the Sun;
- an inherited ancestral object is used;
- the movement of the Sun is changed.

The repetition of these motifs is part of the traditional material.

Their possible physical interpretation is a separate question.

---

## Interpretation in Measurable Science

The Māori traditions themselves do **not** state that the harakeke ropes represent the ether, nor that the jawbone of Murirangawhenua represents technical knowledge.

In *Measurable Science*, the following interpretation is proposed:

**harakeke ropes → ether / connecting medium**

The ropes represent the medium through which separated parts of the system remain connected and through which an action can be transmitted.

**jawbone of the grandmother → inherited knowledge**

The jawbone represents knowledge received from earlier generations: the knowledge required to control or alter the rhythm of the system.

Under this interpretation, the Māui story contains two complementary elements:

**connection + inherited knowledge**

or:

**medium through which the system can be controlled + knowledge of how to control it**

This interpretation is a hypothesis of *Measurable Science*. It is not presented as a literal translation of Māori tradition.

---

## Important Distinction

The Māori sources support the existence and content of the traditions described above.

They document:

- Mauao as originally nameless;
- Pūwhenua and Ōtānewainuku;
- Mauao's grief and intended journey into the Pacific;
- the patupaiarehe;
- the use of ropes;
- the nocturnal journey;
- the karakia **E Hika Tū Ake**;
- Meremere Tūahiahi / Venus;
- Tangaroa and the ocean;
- the arrival of dawn;
- the Waimapu landscape connection;
- Māui and his brothers;
- the excessively short days;
- ropes / nooses made from harakeke;
- the capture and restraint of the Sun;
- the jawbone of Murirangawhenua;
- the slowing of the Sun;
- and the resulting lengthening of the day.

The sources do **not** establish that these stories describe an electromagnetic machine, an ether system, a resonant network or the physical construction of the Sun.

Those are hypotheses investigated separately in *Measurable Science*.

# Sources

## 1. Ngāti Ranginui Iwi — Waimapu Marae

Ngāti Ranginui preserves the tradition of Mauao in the local Tauranga Moana context.

The account describes the nameless mountain, Pūwhenua, Ōtānewainuku, the decision to travel into Te Moananui-a-Kiwa, the patupaiarehe and the hauling of the mountain toward the sea.

The te reo Māori version includes **E Hika Tū Ake**.

English:

https://ngatiranginuittw.co.nz/waimapu-marae/

Te reo Māori:

https://ngatiranginuittw.co.nz/mi/waimapu-marae_reo/

---

## 2. Tauranga City Council — E Hika

Tauranga City Council publishes the Māori text of **E Hika** together with an English translation.

The publication connects the words with the hauling of Mauao along what is now the Waimapu Stream and identifies **Meremere Tūahiahi** as Venus, the Evening Star.

The source also notes that the present melody is relatively recent while the words derive from a very old karakia.

https://www.tauranga.govt.nz/Portals/0/data/culture-community/files/waiata-booklet.pdf

## 3. Mauao Historic Reserve Management Plan — Tauranga City Council

The Mauao Historic Reserve Management Plan contains **Ngā kōrero tūpuna mō Mauao** and records the traditional account and associated karakia in an official local source.
