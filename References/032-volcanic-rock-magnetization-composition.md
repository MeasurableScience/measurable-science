# Reference 032 — Volcanic rock composition and natural remanent magnetization

## Purpose

This reference documents the physical basis used in the book for constructing a working material model of the hypothetical central volcanic structure.

The book does not claim that the composition of Rupes Nigra is known.

The purpose is to determine what material properties would be physically plausible if the structure originated as a very large silicate volcanic jet that was rapidly cooled by water.

---

## Volcanic glass

Volcanic glass forms when silicate melt cools sufficiently rapidly that extensive crystal growth cannot occur.

Rapid interaction between lava and water can therefore produce glassy margins and glass-rich volcanic material.

Basaltic submarine eruptions commonly produce rapidly quenched glass at lava-water interfaces.

This provides a physically known mechanism by which a volcanic structure exposed to large quantities of cold water could develop a dark, glassy external surface.

## Magnetic minerals in basaltic volcanic rocks

Basaltic volcanic rocks commonly contain iron-titanium oxide minerals.

Magnetite and titanomagnetite are particularly important because they can carry natural remanent magnetization.

When volcanic material cools through the relevant magnetic ordering temperatures, these minerals can acquire and preserve a record of the magnetic field existing during cooling.

Very small magnetite particles have also been identified in submarine basaltic glass.

## Typical basalt magnetization

Young oceanic basalt commonly possesses natural remanent magnetization of the order of several amperes per metre.

Values around approximately:

**10 A/m**

provide a reasonable order-of-magnitude reference for strongly magnetized young basaltic crust.

This value is not treated in the book as a universal constant.

Natural remanent magnetization varies substantially with mineralogy, cooling history, oxidation, alteration and geological history.

---

## Exceptionally magnetized Icelandic basalt

Much larger natural remanent magnetizations have been measured in some natural basalt formations.

A particularly relevant example is the Stardalur volcanic complex in Iceland.

Measurements reported for Stardalur basalt include natural remanent magnetizations approximately within the range:

**20–128 A/m**

with a median value of approximately:

**55 A/m**

and a mean around:

**61 A/m**.

These values demonstrate that natural basaltic material can, under particular mineralogical and cooling conditions, possess remanent magnetization far above the values commonly associated with ordinary oceanic basalt.

## Working value used in the book

The composition and magnetic properties of the hypothetical Rupes Nigra cannot be measured because no physical sample is available.

The book therefore does not assign it the maximum magnetization measured in Icelandic basalt.

For the first-order calculation, a deliberately intermediate working value is selected between:

- approximately **10 A/m**, representing the order of magnitude of strongly magnetized young oceanic basalt;

and

- approximately **55 A/m**, representing the median measured value from the unusually strongly magnetized Stardalur basalt in Iceland.

The adopted working value is therefore:

**M = 32.5 A/m**

This is a modelling assumption.

It is not a measured value for Rupes Nigra.

---

## Important limitation concerning geological age

The hypothetical structure considered in the book would, within the author's model, represent an extremely ancient volcanic structure.

Age itself, however, does not imply stronger remanent magnetization.

Hydrothermal alteration, oxidation and mineralogical transformation can substantially modify or reduce the original magnetic signal of basaltic material over time.

Therefore the working value of **32.5 A/m** is not justified by the proposed age of the structure.

It is used only as an intermediate test value between documented ordinary and unusually strongly magnetized natural basalt.

---

## Working material model

Based on known volcanic processes, the first-order hypothetical structure used in the book consists of:

- silicate volcanic material;
- a rapidly quenched glass-bearing external region;
- progressively more crystalline volcanic material toward the interior;
- iron-titanium oxides including magnetite and titanomagnetite;
- natural remanent magnetization carried by magnetic mineral phases.

This combination provides a physically known mechanism capable of producing material that can simultaneously be:

- dark;
- glassy or shiny at rapidly cooled surfaces;
- volcanic;
- magnetically remanent.

The comparison with the historical description of Rupes Nigra is the author's hypothesis.

The geological literature cited here establishes only that these material properties can coexist in natural volcanic rocks.
