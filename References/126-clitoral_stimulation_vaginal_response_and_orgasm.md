
# Clitoral Stimulation, Vaginal Response, and Orgasm

## Purpose

This reference documents four experimental studies relevant to the clitoral-stimulation stage described in Measurable Science.

The studies investigate vaginal vascular responses, the clitorovaginal reflex, pelvic contractions during orgasm, and simultaneous clitoral and vaginal vascular measurements.

They do not investigate the complete sexual sequence proposed in the book.

## 1. Vaginal microcirculation during sexual arousal

Levin, R. J., & Wylie, K. (2008).

Vaginal vasomotion—its appearance, measurement, and usefulness in assessing the mechanisms of vasodilatation.

The Journal of Sexual Medicine.

DOI:
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1743-6109.2007.00669.x

PubMed:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18042218/

### Participants and methods

Fourteen healthy premenopausal women underwent intravaginal infrared photoplethysmography.

The researchers recorded vaginal pulse amplitude (VPA) at rest and during sexual arousal induced by erotic video and/or clitoral stimulation.

### Results

At baseline, the vaginal photoplethysmographic recordings showed pulse waves of different amplitudes.

During sexual arousal, lower-amplitude waves tended to convert into higher-amplitude waves, and higher-amplitude waves generally increased.

The results demonstrated measurable changes in vaginal microcirculation during sexual arousal.

### Limitations

The experiment did not isolate the effects of clitoral stimulation in every participant.

It did not investigate the proposed sequence of kissing, breast stimulation, clitoral orgasm, and subsequent penetration.

## 2. The clitorovaginal reflex

Shafik et al. (2008).

Vaginal response to clitoral stimulation: identification of the clitorovaginal reflex.

PubMed:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18357802/

### Participants and methods

Twenty-six healthy women participated.

The researchers applied electrical and mechanical stimulation to the glans clitoris.

Vaginal wall electromyographic activity and vaginal pressure were recorded.

The experiment was repeated after separate local anesthesia of the clitoris and vagina, as well as after application of a non-anesthetic gel.

### Results

Clitoral stimulation significantly increased vaginal electrical activity and pressure (p < 0.01).

The response was absent after local anesthesia of either the clitoris or vagina.

The authors interpreted the results as evidence of a clitorovaginal excitatory reflex.

### Limitations

The experiment demonstrated a physiological response to controlled stimulation.

It did not establish that the same response necessarily occurs with identical intensity during every sexual encounter or that it guarantees orgasm.

## 3. Pelvic contractions during female orgasm

Bohlen, J. G., Held, J. P., Sanderson, M. O., & Ahlgren, A. (1982).

The female orgasm: pelvic contractions.

Archives of Sexual Behavior, 11, 367–386.

DOI:
https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01541570

PubMed:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7181645/

### Participants and methods

Eleven women who had not given birth participated.

Each woman manually stimulated herself to orgasm on three separate occasions.

Vaginal and anal pressure were measured simultaneously using two probes.

### Results

Nine women displayed a series of regular contractions near the perceived beginning of orgasm.

Anal and vaginal contraction waveforms were synchronized, with equal numbers of contractions in both locations.

The perceived beginning of orgasm did not always correspond precisely to the onset of regular contractions.

The mean intervals between successive contractions increased progressively through the contraction sequence.

### Limitations

The sample was small.

The study measured pelvic pressure and subjective orgasm onset. It did not simultaneously record all relevant brain, hormonal, cardiovascular, and vascular responses.

The findings do not identify a single neural mechanism that initiates orgasm.

## 4. Simultaneous clitoral and vaginal vascular measurements

Mechelmans, D. J., Sachtler, W. L., von Wiegand, T. E., Goodrich, D., Heiman, J. R., & Janssen, E. (2020).

The Successful Measurement of Clitoral Pulse Amplitude Using a New Clitoral Photoplethysmograph: A Pilot Study.

The Journal of Sexual Medicine, 17(6), 1118–1125.

DOI:
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsxm.2020.02.017

Full text:
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7282935/

### Participants and methods

Fifteen healthy, sexually functional premenopausal women participated.

Researchers simultaneously measured clitoral pulse amplitude (CPA) and vaginal pulse amplitude (VPA).

Participants watched erotic, anxiety-inducing, and neutral video excerpts and reported subjective sexual arousal.

### Results

The new clitoral photoplethysmograph successfully recorded clitoral pulse amplitude.

Both clitoral and vaginal signals responded to erotic stimulation.

The clitoral signal more closely followed changes in the intensity of erotic content than the vaginal signal.

Correlations between genital measurements and subjective arousal were generally higher for the clitoral measure.

### Limitations

This was a small pilot study involving healthy volunteers.

The stimuli were audiovisual, not direct clitoral stimulation.

The study did not investigate orgasm or the proposed sequence described in Measurable Science.

Clitoral and vaginal pulse amplitudes cannot be directly compared as absolute quantities because the instruments use different measurement systems.

## Evidence classification

### MEASUREMENT

- Vaginal photoplethysmographic changes during sexual arousal.
- Increased vaginal electromyographic activity and pressure following clitoral stimulation.
- Synchronized vaginal and anal contractions during orgasm in nine of eleven participants.
- Simultaneous clitoral and vaginal vascular responses to erotic audiovisual stimuli.

### MATHEMATICAL ANALYSIS

- Vaginal pulse-wave classification and amplitude analysis.
- Statistical comparison of vaginal EMG activity and pressure.
- Timing and synchronization of pelvic contraction waveforms.
- Statistical analysis of vascular signals and subjective arousal.

### HYPOTHESIS

The book proposes that increasing sexual stimulation eventually produces a coordinated physiological transition involving multiple bodily systems.

The cited studies document individual components of this process.

They do not demonstrate that every physiological system changes simultaneously or establish a single triggering mechanism.

A direct test would require synchronized recordings of neural, cardiovascular, genital vascular, muscular, and hormonal responses during the same experimental session.

## Conclusion

Clitoral stimulation can produce measurable vaginal responses, while orgasm can involve synchronized pelvic contractions.

Different genital vascular measurements also reveal distinct temporal response patterns.

These experiments establish separate physiological observations relevant to the proposed method, but they do not validate its complete sequence or final objective.
