# Reference 043 — Ancient traditions of the sky as a firmament, boundary or structured heaven

## Purpose

This reference documents a widespread historical and mythological motif:

ancient cultures across widely separated regions repeatedly described the sky
not as an undefined empty void, but as a structured upper boundary, roof,
vault, canopy, firmament, layered heaven, or supported celestial domain.

The traditions are not identical.

They use different materials, geometries, divine figures and symbolic language.

This reference therefore does NOT claim that all twelve traditions describe
the same physical object in exactly the same way.

It documents the recurring structural idea:

# a bounded or structured heaven exists above the human world.

The physical interpretation proposed elsewhere in the book is the author's
hypothesis.

---

# 1. Mesopotamia — Sumerian, Babylonian and Assyrian traditions

A particularly clear source is the Babylonian creation epic:

**Enūma Eliš**

In Tablet IV, after defeating Tiamat, Marduk divides her body.

One half is placed above to form the sky.

A widely used translation by Stephanie Dalley renders the passage:

"Half of her he put up to roof the sky,
Drew a bolt across and made a guard hold it.
Her waters he arranged so that they could not escape."

The imagery is explicitly architectural:

- a roof is made above;
- it is secured;
- waters are retained beyond it.

Another translation published in Alexander Heidel's
*The Babylonian Genesis* renders the same passage:

"Half of her he set in place and formed the sky
(therewith) as a roof.
He fixed the crossbar and posted guards;
He commanded them not to let her waters escape."

This provides direct textual evidence for a conceived boundary separating
the inhabited cosmos from waters beyond the sky.

# 2. Ancient Hebrew tradition — rāqîaʿ

Genesis 1 describes the creation of:

**rāqîaʿ**

between waters.

Its stated function is:

to separate waters below from waters above.

Genesis 1:6–8 therefore presents a distinct boundary between two bodies
of water.

The traditional Greek translation uses:

**stereōma**

and the Latin Vulgate uses:

**firmamentum**

for this structure.

The Brown–Driver–Briggs Hebrew lexicon describes rāqîaʿ as an:

"extended surface, (solid) expanse"

and interprets the heavenly rāqîaʿ as the vault or firmament supporting
waters above.

Paul H. Seely's historical-linguistic study likewise argues that the
original conception was of a solid firmament rather than simply an
atmospheric expanse.

There is modern scholarly disagreement on whether Genesis requires a
literally solid dome.

That disagreement should be acknowledged.

The point relevant here is narrower:

Genesis explicitly describes a structure separating waters below from
waters above, and a major historical and lexicographical tradition has
understood that structure as a firmament or solid expanse.

# 3. Ancient Egypt

Ancient Egyptian representations repeatedly portray the sky as an
organized structure above the earthly world.

The sky goddess:

**Nut**

is commonly shown arched over the earth.

James Evans, in *The History and Practice of Ancient Astronomy*, notes
that Egyptian art often represented Nut arched above the land and that
the Egyptian hieroglyph for "sky" resembles the flat roof of a building.

Astronomical images were also placed on the ceilings of coffins, tombs
and temples, reinforcing the architectural representation of the sky
as an upper cosmic domain.

The exact material composition attributed to the Egyptian sky varied
between texts and periods.

The robust point is the persistent conception of a distinct structured
heaven above the earth.

# 4. Ancient Greece

Archaic Greek poetry contains repeated references to a:

**bronze heaven**

Greek:

χάλκεος οὐρανός

or related formulations.

Homeric texts employ metallic imagery for heaven, and later Greek
poetry continues the motif.

Filippomaria Pontani documents the recurring "bronze heaven" formula
in archaic Greek poetry and discusses its meaning as a boundary
separating gods and mortals.

The broader Greek tradition also developed the concept of the celestial
sphere and stories involving Atlas in relation to heaven.

# 5. Ancient Rome and the Latin firmamentum

Roman and later Latin tradition inherited much of Greek celestial
language and cosmology.

Of particular importance is the Latin word:

# firmamentum

Lewis and Short define its basic meaning as:

- strengthening;
- support;
- prop.

In later Latin it is also used for:

"the sky fixed above the earth, the firmament."

The Latin Vulgate famously uses:

**firmamentum**

for the rāqîaʿ of Genesis.

The modern English word:

**firmament**

ultimately derives from this Latin tradition.

# 6. Ancient China

Ancient China developed several competing cosmological models.

One of the oldest is:

# Gài Tiān — "Canopy Heaven"

In this model heaven is conceived in relation to the earth through the
image of a canopy or covering.

A scholarly analysis of the early Chinese model notes the comparison
with the round canopy of a chariot and quotes the *Kao Gong Ji*:

"The squareness of the chariot is to represent Earth;
the roundness of the canopy is to represent heaven."

The model also assigns a definite distance between earth and heaven.

Chinese cosmology was not uniform — alternative models such as
Hun Tian later existed — but Gai Tian provides clear evidence of an
ancient conception of heaven as a structured covering above earth.

# 7. Maya and Aztec / broader Mesoamerican traditions

Mesoamerican cosmologies contain unusually clear traditions of
sky-supporting structures associated with the four directions.

Among the Yucatec Maya, the:

# Bacabs

are four beings associated with the four directions.

Traditional accounts describe them as being placed at the four points
of the world:

"holding up the sky so that it should not fall."

Maya literary traditions therefore preserve the concept of four
sky-bearers.

Mexican traditions contain a closely related motif.

A historical Mexican source describes four gods creating four routes or
supports in order to:

**raise the heavens**

after heaven had fallen upon the earth.

Thus both Maya and central Mexican traditions contain:

- an upper heaven;
- separation of heaven and earth;
- four directional supports or bearers.

# 8. Polynesian and Māori traditions

Māori cosmology preserves one of the clearest sky-separation traditions.

Ranginui:

**Sky Father**

and Papatūānuku:

**Earth Mother**

were originally joined together.

Their children existed confined in darkness between them.

Tāne separated the two.

Ranginui was raised upward and the space between heaven and earth was
opened.

The tradition therefore contains explicitly:

- sky and earth originally joined;
- darkness between them;
- deliberate separation;
- sky elevated above the earth;
- the opening of the inhabited world between the two.

Versions of the wider Polynesian tradition additionally describe the
raising or propping up of the sky.

### Sources

Te Ara — The Encyclopedia of New Zealand.

Māori creation traditions.

https://teara.govt.nz/

Additional textual overview:

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rangi_and_Papa

Primary and historical Māori collections should be preferred where
possible when quoting the story directly.

# 9. Slavic traditions

The evidence for a single unified "Slavic cosmology" is less direct than
for Genesis, Enūma Eliš or the Eddas because much early Slavic mythology
was preserved through later folklore rather than a single ancient
cosmogonic manuscript.

Nevertheless, Slavic folk material preserves a vertically organized
cosmos comprising:

- sky / heaven;
- earth;
- underworld.

Research on South Slavic folklore additionally records traditions in
which stars and constellations are described as being:

**fastened or nailed to the lowest layer of the firmament.**

Slavic traditional imagery also preserves the idea of the sky/heaven
as a distinct celestial level and of a world axis connecting earth and
heaven.

The evidence therefore supports a structured upper celestial domain,
although the exact geometry varies between Slavic traditions.

# 10. Norse tradition

Norse cosmology provides an exceptionally explicit material description.

In the creation account preserved in the Eddic tradition, the gods kill
Ymir and construct the world from his body.

His skull becomes:

# the sky.

The sky is placed above the newly ordered world.

Thus the heavenly structure is not merely metaphorically "above" the
world.

Within the narrative it is given a material origin from Ymir's body.

The Eddic cosmos also contains multiple distinct regions organized above,
within and below the inhabited world.

### Sources

Snorri Sturluson.

*Prose Edda — Gylfaginning.*

Public-domain translations are available through:

https://www.sacred-texts.com/neu/pre/

The motif is also catalogued in comparative folklore as:

"origin of sky from Ymir's skull."

# 11. Indigenous traditions of North America

There was no single North American Indigenous cosmology.

Hundreds of nations preserved different creation traditions.

Nevertheless, several documented traditions contain conceptions of an
upper sky world, sky boundary, dome or firmament.

A nineteenth-century Smithsonian study of North American Indigenous
mythology records, for example, a Shoshoni conception of a:

**domed firmament**

and discusses additional traditions concerning the construction or
organization of the sky.

Iroquoian traditions also preserve the concept of:

**Sky World**

above the human earth, from which the Sky Woman descends in the creation
narrative.

These examples should not be generalized to every Indigenous nation.

They establish that the conception of a distinct upper celestial world
or boundary occurred independently in multiple North American traditions.

# 12. Ethiopia — the Ethiopic tradition of 1 Enoch

The Ethiopian tradition is particularly important because the complete
text of:

# 1 Enoch

was preserved in Ge'ez and remains canonical in the Ethiopian Orthodox
Tewahedo Church.

The cosmological portions of 1 Enoch repeatedly describe structured
boundaries of heaven.

The text includes:

- the firmament of heaven;
- ends of heaven and earth;
- heavenly gates;
- pillars of heaven;
- winds associated with supporting the firmament.

A traditional textual reading of 1 Enoch 18 describes:

**four winds which bear the earth and the firmament of heaven**

and refers to the:

**pillars of heaven.**

The astronomical sections additionally describe gates through which the
Sun and Moon complete their courses.

The imagery is therefore explicitly architectural and spatial:

- firmament;
- pillars;
- edges;
- gates;
- ordered paths of celestial lights.

### Source

Charles, R. H.

*The Book of Enoch or 1 Enoch.*

Text and critical commentary.

An accessible scholarly scan:

https://www.crcnh.org/downloads/bible-study-tools/enoch/The-Book-of-Enoch-Expert-Analysis.pdf

The commentary notes the cosmological conception that:

the ends of the firmament of heaven rest on the ends of the earth,

and identifies the winds of 1 Enoch 18 with the support of the heavenly
vault.

For the Ethiopic manuscript tradition and canonical status, see standard
studies of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo biblical canon.

# Comparative summary

The twelve traditions examined here are:

1. Mesopotamian
2. Ancient Hebrew
3. Egyptian
4. Greek
5. Roman / Latin
6. Chinese
7. Maya and Aztec / Mesoamerican
8. Polynesian and Māori
9. Slavic
10. Norse
11. Indigenous North American
12. Ethiopian / Ethiopic Enochic

They do not describe one identical cosmological diagram.

However, recurring motifs include:

- a sky conceived as a distinct upper structure;
- heaven separated from earth;
- a roof, canopy, vault, firmament or layered celestial region;
- waters or another realm beyond the heavenly boundary;
- pillars, bearers or supports;
- gates or openings in the sky;
- celestial lights associated with the upper structure;
- directional organization, frequently involving four directions.

The recurrence of these motifs across geographically and historically
separated traditions is the historical fact documented by this reference.

# Important limitation

This reference does NOT establish that a physical plasma firmament exists
above the modern Earth.

It also does NOT establish that all ancient traditions inherited a single
historical observation.

Several explanations are possible for similarities between ancient
cosmologies, including:

- common human observation of the visible sky;
- independent symbolic development;
- cultural transmission;
- shared mythological inheritance;
- or an underlying physical phenomenon.

Determining between those possibilities is outside the scope of this
reference.

The book investigates the final possibility as an author's hypothesis.

The historical claim supported here is narrower:

# ancient traditions across many regions repeatedly described the sky as
# a structured boundary or celestial domain above the human world,
# rather than simply as an undefined empty void.
