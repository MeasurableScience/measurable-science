## 2. Christian hesychasm — silence and the prayer of the heart

Hesychasm is an Eastern Orthodox contemplative practice involving inner silence, sustained attention, and prayer.

The Jesus Prayer is repeated continuously. Hesychastic teaching describes the gathering of the mind into the heart and the development of the prayer of the heart.

Solitude is an important element in the lives of many hesychast monks. Historical examples include withdrawal into monastic cells, hermitages, and caves.

**Church sources**

Orthodox Church in America — "The Jesus Prayer."

https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/spirituality/prayer-fasting-and-almsgiving/the-jesus-prayer

Explains the repeated Jesus Prayer, the hesychast method, inner silence, and the relationship between attention, mind, and heart.

Orthodox Church in America — "Unceasing Prayer."

https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/spirituality/prayer-fasting-and-almsgiving/unceasing-prayer

Explains the Orthodox tradition of continuous prayer.

Orthodox Church in America — "Saint Gregory Palamas, Archbishop of Thessalonica."

https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/1002/11/14/103303-saint-gregory-palamas-archbishop-of-thessalonica

Describes hesychasm as mental prayer requiring solitude and quiet. Gregory spent five days each week in silence and prayer, emerging on Saturdays and Sundays.

Orthodox Church in America — "Venerable Macarius the Great of Egypt."

https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2015/01/19/100226-venerable-macarius-the-great-of-egypt

Describes how Macarius dug a deep cave beneath his monastic cell and withdrew there for prayer and meditation.

Orthodox Church in America — "Fasting."

https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/spirituality/prayer-fasting-and-almsgiving/fasting

Explains fasting as a spiritual discipline associated with prayer, self-control, and freedom from passions.

These sources document silence, repetition, solitude, cells, and caves. They do not establish that complete darkness is a universal requirement of hesychasm.

## 3. Hindu mantra — repetition of sacred sound

Mantra is a sacred sound, word, or formula repeated aloud or mentally.

Different Hindu traditions use mantra for devotion, concentration, contemplation, and spiritual development.

**Source**

Encyclopaedia Britannica — "Mantra."

https://www.britannica.com/topic/mantra

This provides a parallel with the repeated Jesus Prayer at the level of repetition and concentrated attention, without equating their religious meanings.

## 4. Buddhism — samatha and vipassanā

Samatha refers to calming and stabilizing the mind. Vipassanā involves insight developed through attentive observation.

In the Vipassana tradition taught by S. N. Goenka, students first develop concentration through observation of natural breathing. They then systematically observe bodily sensations without reacting to them.

Pleasant, unpleasant, and neutral sensations are observed with equanimity, recognizing their changing nature.

**Official Vipassana sources**

Vipassana Meditation — official website.

https://www.dhamma.org/en/about/vipassana

Explains Vipassana as self-transformation through self-observation and describes its ten-day residential courses.

Vipassana Meditation — "Guidelines for Practicing."

https://www.dhamma.org/en/osguide

Provides the practical distinction between observing breathing and observing bodily sensations. It explicitly describes maintaining equanimity toward pleasant, unpleasant, and neutral sensations.

Vipassana Meditation — "Code of Discipline."

https://www.dhamma.org/en/about/code

Describes the structure of the residential course, including silence, moral discipline, concentration, and meditation.

**Early Buddhist source**

*Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta*, Majjhima Nikāya 10.

https://accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.010.than

Describes observation of the body, feelings, mind, and mental phenomena, including the recognition of pleasant, painful, and neutral feelings.

### My interpretation

I compare sustained observation with the establishment of a stable reference point from which changes can be observed.

The term "inert reference point" belongs to my model, not to the Buddhist sources.

## 5. Daoist internal alchemy — jing, qi, and shen

Daoist internal alchemy, or *neidan*, describes processes of inner cultivation and transformation.

Its traditional vocabulary includes:

jing — essence

qi — vital breath

shen — spirit

The refinement of essence, vital breath, and spirit is an important theme in internal alchemical traditions.

**Sources**

Fabrizio Pregadio — *The Encyclopedia of Taoism*, Routledge.

https://www.routledge.com/The-Encyclopedia-of-Taoism/Pregadio/p/book/9780415678155

Fabrizio Pregadio — *The Way of the Golden Elixir: An Introduction to Taoist Alchemy*.

https://www.goldenelixir.com/press/tao_01_golden_elixir.html

These sources provide historical and doctrinal context for Daoist internal alchemy.

The terms jing, qi, and shen are traditional concepts. Their identification with particular experimentally measurable forms of energy would require separate evidence.

## 6. Haṭha Yoga and tantric traditions — bindu and transformation

The *Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā* describes bodily and contemplative practices associated with liberation and overcoming death.

Its third chapter includes teachings about mudrā, kuṇḍalinī, and the preservation of bindu.

The Vajrolī section explicitly connects the preservation of bindu with overcoming death.

**Primary source**

*Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā*, Chapter III.

https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hatha_Yoga_Pradipika/3

Relevant sections:

- The Mudras
- The Vajroli
- The Sakti Chalana

This source establishes that the connection between bindu and overcoming death appears in classical Haṭha Yoga literature.

It does not establish that preserving reproductive fluids produces biological immortality. Nor does this one text represent every tantric tradition.

## 7. Three practices identified in Measurable Science

After comparing these sources, I distinguish three practical principles.

**Repetition — rhythm**

The repeated Jesus Prayer and Hindu mantra provide examples of sustained verbal or mental repetition.

**Stillness and observation — reference point**

Hesychasm emphasizes inner silence and attention. Buddhist meditation provides explicit methods of observing changing experiences without reacting to them.

I interpret these practices through the concept of a stable reference point.

**Preservation and transformation**

Daoist internal alchemy and certain Haṭha Yoga traditions describe the refinement or preservation of vital essence.

I compare these teachings with the third principle developed in the manuscript: transformation.

The sources document the existence and traditional purposes of these practices. My proposed relationship between them, and their possible role in a larger natural information-processing system, remains a hypothesis.
