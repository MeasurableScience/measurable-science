# Reference 107 — Four Magistrales and Synchronization Geometry

## Purpose

This document gives the complete mathematical derivation of the synchronization resultant used in *Measurable Science*.

The important methodological point is that the positions of the Tropics are not used as an input to this calculation.

The calculation begins with the previously defined geometry of the four unequal magistrales and the MASTER–CPU–FEEDBACK system. The resulting amplitude is calculated first and only then compared with the geographical positions of the Tropics.

---

## 1. Four magistrales

On the north-centered azimuthal projection, the four magistrales have the following directions:

M₁ = 60°

M₂ = 130°

M₃ = 210°

M₄ = 335°

Their unequal geometry produces a combined phase-dependent resultant.

After resolving the four magistrales into Cartesian components, the combined vector is:

X(φ) = −0.091782 cos φ + 0.109382 sin φ

Y(φ) = 0.109382 cos φ + 0.091782 sin φ

where φ is the phase of the system.

---

## 2. Magnitude of the resultant

The magnitude of the resultant is:

R² = X² + Y²

Substituting the component equations:

R² =
(−0.091782 cos φ + 0.109382 sin φ)²
+
(0.109382 cos φ + 0.091782 sin φ)²

Expanding both terms gives:

R² =
0.091782² cos²φ
− 2(0.091782)(0.109382) cosφ sinφ
+ 0.109382² sin²φ

+

0.109382² cos²φ
+ 2(0.109382)(0.091782) cosφ sinφ
+ 0.091782² sin²φ

The two mixed terms cancel:

−2ab cosφ sinφ + 2ab cosφ sinφ = 0

Therefore:

R² =
(0.091782² + 0.109382²)
(cos²φ + sin²φ)

Since:

cos²φ + sin²φ = 1

the expression reduces to:

R² = 0.091782² + 0.109382²

and therefore:

R = √(0.091782² + 0.109382²)

R ≈ 0.142788

Thus:

R = 0.142788

The magnitude is independent of φ.

---

## 3. Why the resultant describes a circle

The phase changes the direction of the resultant but not its magnitude.

Its direction can be written approximately as:

θ(φ) ≈ 130° − φ

Therefore, as φ passes through a complete cycle from 0° to 360°, the endpoint of the resultant rotates through 360° while remaining at the constant radius:

R = 0.142788

The geometrical result is therefore a circle, not an ellipse.

A quarter-cycle produces a 90° rotation:

φ → φ + 90°
θ → θ − 90°

A half-cycle produces the opposite state:

φ → φ + 180°
θ → θ − 180°

and after a complete cycle:

φ → φ + 360°
θ → θ − 360°

the system returns to its initial geometrical state.

## 4. MASTER–FEEDBACK axis

The previously calculated distance between MASTER and FEEDBACK is approximately:

D = 18,117 km

Expressed as a great-circle angular separation, this is approximately:

Dₐ ≈ 162.92°

This provides the angular scale onto which the dimensionless geometrical resultant can be projected.

The maximum displacement from the central reference position is therefore:

A = Dₐ × R

A = 162.92° × 0.142788

A ≈ 23.263°

Thus the geometrical model produces the range:

−23.263° ↔ 0° ↔ +23.263°

or, rounded:

±23.26°

## 5. Comparison with the Tropics

Only after obtaining the geometrical result above is it compared with the observed geographical positions of the Tropics.

Approximate present value:

Tropic of Cancer ≈ +23.44°

Tropic of Capricorn ≈ −23.44°

Calculated model amplitude:

A ≈ ±23.26°

Difference in absolute latitude:

Δ = 23.44° − 23.263°

Δ ≈ 0.177°

Relative difference:

Δrel = 0.177 / 23.44 × 100

Δrel ≈ 0.76%

Therefore:

Model geometry: ±23.26°
Tropics:        ±23.44°
Difference:      0.18° ≈ 0.76%

---

## 6. Interpretation within the model

The value ±23.26° is not obtained by inserting the latitude of the Tropics into the calculation.

The sequence is:

four unequal magistrales
→ geometrical vector components
→ constant resultant R = 0.142788
→ circular phase rotation
→ MASTER–FEEDBACK angular scale ≈ 162.92°
→ displacement ≈ ±23.26°
→ comparison with the Tropics ≈ ±23.44°

The comparison with the Tropics is therefore performed after the geometrical result has been obtained.

Within the model presented in *Measurable Science*, this displacement represents the range of the global synchronization point.

It does not represent the physical trajectory of one Sun across the entire Earth. The global synchronization point acts as a phase address from which local regions can form their local solar points.
