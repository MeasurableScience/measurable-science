## Potassium-40

Potassium-40 (K-40) is a naturally occurring radioactive isotope of potassium.

It contains:

- 19 protons
- 21 neutrons
- mass number 40

Its half-life is approximately 1.25 billion years.

Natural potassium contains approximately 0.012% K-40.

K-40 decays primarily by beta-minus decay to calcium-40 and by electron capture to argon-40.

The electron-capture branch is associated with characteristic gamma radiation of approximately 1.46 MeV.

## Primordial significance

Long-lived naturally occurring radionuclides that have persisted in terrestrial matter include K-40, Rb-87 and the long-lived uranium and thorium radionuclides.

Among these long-lived primordial radionuclides naturally present in terrestrial matter, K-40 has the lowest mass number.

This distinction is important.

K-40 is not the lightest radioactive isotope known. Lighter radioactive isotopes exist.

The statement refers specifically to long-lived primordial radionuclides naturally retained in terrestrial matter.

## Relevance to the book

The book examines K-40 as the first low-mass example of nuclear instability encountered when primordial terrestrial radionuclides are considered in order of mass number.

Its significance to the author's model is interpretative:

a material structure can exist for immense periods while still remaining internally unstable and undergoing transformation.

K-40 therefore provides a measurable example of the principle explored in the book:

instability → change → transformation → new form + released energy

The subsequent interpretation of this instability as part of a wider sequence of material transformation is the author's model, not an established conclusion of nuclear physics.
